I · Welcome

Welcome to the Omani Night Sky

In parts of northern Oman, stars are still used to time the sharing of irrigation water, even though watches are everywhere. Farmers watch certain stars rise, and each rising marks a slice of the night. Many of the stars carry local names that differ from those in books on classical Arabic stars.

Tap a star below. These are some of the stars used in the village of Qaryah Beni Subh, and the length of the time slices (āthār) each one marks.

Choose a star

Each star has an Omani name, an international name, and a place in the night’s schedule.

A night of 48 slices

A day is divided into forty-eight āthār. By day, an athar is the time a shadow takes to lengthen by a man’s foot, nominally half an hour. A sundial of poles and painted lines tells it.

Stars that move

Each evening the stars rise four minutes earlier, so different stars serve in different seasons. One painted chart from Mudayrib shows twenty-four stars across a year, six in each quarter.

Many names, one star

Al‑Shaʿaraʾ is usually Sirius, but Procyon is also called al‑Shaʿaraʾ. Stars called by body parts, such as al‑Janb (the side), appear in many constellations.

The Star Ledger of Qaryah Beni Subh

Twenty‑one main stars are used here, and with divider stars the total is about fifty. The gap between main stars ranges from 40 to 90 minutes, split into āthār of 20 to 50 minutes. These are the stars identified so far, with the minutes of each athar between one star and the next. The source marks some times as not yet verified.

Divider stars

Between main stars, unnamed “divider” stars (manṣaf) help split the gap. Betelgeuse divides al‑Shubcha and al‑Jawzaʾ, and Pollux divides al‑Jawzaʾ and al‑Shaʿaraʾ. Between al‑Dhakarayn and al‑Ghafur, two dividers fall 20 minutes apart, each rising 20 minutes after the one before. Some dividers do not match the athar times at all.

One horizon

In Qaryah Beni Subh, stars are watched rising above a fixed horizon from three stargazing points, so farm workers can reach one wherever they are irrigating. The official stargazer, Rashid bin Khamis al‑Subhi, stays out all night. His father held the post before him, and he is teaching it to his brother and son.

A moving observer

In Barzamān a wall and in Sudayrah palm trees mark the horizon. The observer steps forward, pausing at points where the star will rise one athar or one sahm later than at the last point.

Walls, posts and the zenith

Zahib uses about forty viewing points along an alley, with a horizontal pole as the main marker. Stars are watched rising above it from the west and setting from the east, and al‑Nisr is watched almost overhead. In al‑Fath, an observer looks west from a mosque wall, moving back so a star sets later each time.

Twenty‑four stars, a year of nights

The Muda’ybi area uses twenty‑four stars, with minor differences between villages. A chart painted in Mudayrib in the 1980s shows six stars in each season, with the minutes between them in star shapes around the rim.

Names that change

Some names differ by village. In the Beni Subh area the “k” often becomes “ch”, so al‑Chūī and al‑Shubcha are also called al‑Kūī and al‑Shubka. Al‑Jawzaʾ is Alhena, and the Mudayrib chart’s al‑Ẓalmī may be the same star. At Zahib, the timing star was changed from Procyon to Sirius some forty years ago to suit farm workers from the Indian sub‑continent.

II · The Sea

How Omanis Used Stars to Navigate

Navigators at sea measured how high a star stood above the horizon, not in degrees but in finger‑widths. Because most northern Indian Ocean routes lie within 25° of the equator, the Pole Star sits close to the horizon and can be measured accurately. The more fingers it measured, the farther north the ship.

The Kamal and the “wood”

A simple tool that lets sailors measure the angle of stars above the horizon. By the fifteenth century, wooden instruments called a khashaba replaced the hand to standardize readings.

Stellar compass

Compass points carried star names: “northeast” was “the rising of Capella” and “northwest” “the setting of Capella.” Ibn Majid’s wind‑rose had 32 rhumbs.

The “Jah” star

A log entry that places Muscat at “Jah 5” means the star stands five finger‑widths above the horizon there. Checking it every 24 hours gave the captain his position.

A calendar of numbers

Sea communities split the year into 36 parts called Dar, using numbers. Desert and town dwellers named star risings, called Naw.

Masters of the craft

Ahmad ibn Majid and Sulayman al‑Mahri wrote the great navigation texts. Ibn Majid listed over 73 star combinations for altitude measurement. Al‑Mahri gave over 30 set routes, such as Muscat to Malacca.

Riding the monsoon

Seasonal winds blow northeast in winter and southwest in summer. Sailors timed departures to them: crossing the Bay of Bengal meant leaving in late April or May.

III · The Oasis

How People Found and Managed Water

Oman’s aflāj (singular falaj) are channels that tap groundwater and carry it to fields by gravity alone. A tunnel is dug at a shallow angle to reach the water table, with access shafts for removing spoil and for ventilation. About 4,000 systems were counted in the late 1990s, and about 3,000 were still in use. The oldest known examples date back to the third millennium BC.

Water flows day and night, so its use is divided by time. Each farmer’s share is the time the water runs to their field. Press each step to see where the water goes:

Why a falaj never runs dry by greed

A falaj is a sustainable way to use water, because the flow to the fields cannot exceed the groundwater that is recharged. The water is often taken from wadi gravel deposits, which store more and give more reliable yields than bedrock.

A word that means “share”

In classical Arabic, falaj carries meanings such as “to divide into shares” and “running water.” In Oman it names the whole system: tapping groundwater, bringing it to the surface and dividing it among fields by gravity.

Thousands of years of channels

At least six systems dated to about 1000 BC have been identified in the UAE and Oman, and one near Bahla is attributed to the third millennium BC. Irrigated land peaked in the late Sasanian period, and another wave of building and repair came in the mid‑seventeenth to mid‑eighteenth centuries. Some systems reach more than 100 km south of the mountains, into areas with almost no rain.

A shared effort

Building and repair were once carried out or paid for by the falaj community. Today most can afford only routine upkeep such as cleaning silt from channels. Pumped wells have lowered groundwater and dried up some falaj, though the government funds repairs and even support wells.

The day and the night

The day is split into two big blocks (badah), and into forty‑eight āthār. Daytime is read from a sundial, a marker pole and lines running roughly north–south. Some villages use the sahm of 45 minutes instead. The smallest practical slice for irrigation is one twenty‑fourth of an athar, a little over a minute.

Before the stars took over

In Misfat al‑Abriyin, stars were used for only about 115 years, until the 1970s, once the falaj was extended and reorganized. Earlier, with a small area to cultivate, water could simply flood the land. Elsewhere a water clock was used, as formerly in Nizwa, and in some mountain villages.

Sharing without a chief

In the Muda’ybi area there are no official stargazers. People take turns when their water is due, with one or two knowledgeable people teaching the method and settling disputes.

IV · The Fading

Why This Traditional Knowledge Is Disappearing

Of about fifteen aflāj visited in March 2005, stars were used for only one. Below, each glowing light is one falaj. Tap a light to see how few still read the stars.

Keepers are aging

The people who know the system are dying out, and many who remain have failing eyesight. In Mintirib, stargazing ended when its main stargazer died in 1995.

Hard to learn

The knowledge passes by word of mouth. Few are interested, and many who might have done it now have an education and seek better‑paid work.

Watches arrived

Oman opened to the outside world around 1970, and watches soon followed. In several villages stars and sun were dropped in the 1970s. On cloudy nights, even stargazing villages check watches.

Turn up the lights

Light pollution is reducing the visibility of the stars. Drag to brighten the village and watch the sky fade.

V · The Question

Should We Preserve This Heritage?

All knowledge of this art may soon be lost. Researchers argue it is important to the cultural heritage of Oman and the Arabian Peninsula to record where, how, and why stars are still used. There is hope too: in Zahib, expatriate farm workers new to the village learn and use the system. No detailed knowledge of astronomy is needed, nor is reading. Experience and attentiveness to detail are enough.

Record the method

Identify the stars and survey the walls, posts and markers used, in enough detail to replicate them even if the buildings are destroyed.

Take an inventory

A national cultural inventory of falaj systems is beyond one researcher and should be carried out by the interested ministries.

Keep it living

Stargazing can continue, apart from light pollution, if the people who use the falaj want it to.
